27B at 30 tok/s on a $565 32GB server GPU — no CUDA

TL;DR: Qwen3.8-27B at Q6_K, 192K context, 30.5–32.2 tok/s decode on a Radeon Pro V620 — 32 GB of AMD VRAM for about $565 — running llama.cpp on ROCm with MTP speculative decoding at 92% draft acceptance. Six probe runs, every number below is from the server's own timing report. Here are the numbers, the exact flags, and the four traps that cost me a month.

The card

The Radeon Pro V620 is a virtual-desktop GPU from the exadatacenter flood: Navi 21 silicon (same family as the RX 6800 XT, launched 2020) with 32 GB of GDDR6 instead of 16, no display outputs, and passive cooling — it expects a server chassis with an air duct, which is exactly what I gave it.

When I bought mine, they were about $300. They were $565 when I wrote the first draft of this. Today they're going for about $1,000 — the internet found them, which is the entire arc of these cards and the reason the timing was luck rather than skill. If you're reading this in 2027 and they're still four figures, enjoy the numbers anyway; the point was never that it stayed cheap.

What you don't get: CUDA, display outputs, a cooler, or official ROCm support.
What you do get: the cheapest 32 GB of AMD VRAM on the planet — which means the cheapest way to run a 27B Q6 model with a real context window instead of a toy one.

And before the comments ask: yes, the whole machine lives in a cardboard box, held together with zip ties and a $4 tube of silicone sealant. Every component inside costs what it costs; none of it costs what the branding around it costs. The RGB tax is a tax. The tempered-glass tax is a tax. My junction temps are measured, my airflow is engineered, and my chassis cost $0 — the box is not a compromise, it is the thesis.

The flags that matter

llama-server \
  --model Qwen3.8-27B-UD-Q6_K.gguf \
  --ctx-size 196608 \
  --n-gpu-layers 99 \
  --flash-attn auto \
  -ctk q8_0 -ctv q8_0 \
  --spec-type draft-mtp --spec-draft-n-max 2 \
  --batch-size 2048 --ubatch-size 512 \
  --parallel 1

Three of those lines are doing all the work:

  • --spec-type draft-mtp — Qwen3.8 GGUFs ship with a Multi-Token Prediction head (blk.64.nextn.*) baked in. llama.cpp drives it as a speculative decoder: 36 draft tokens proposed, 33 accepted (92%) in every single probe run. This is the biggest speed lever on the box, and it's a llama.cpp feature, not a CUDA kernel — AMD gets it for free.
  • -ctk q8_0 -ctv q8_0 — KV-cache quantization. The 192K context fits in 32 GB because the cache is quantized. Without it, the model and its context are enemies.
  • --ctx-size 196608 — the point of 32 GB. A 27B model that holds an entire codebase, an entire conversation, an entire incident post-mortem without amnesia.

The build (and the ROCm trap)

llama.cpp with -DGGML_HIP=ON, HIP architectures set to the card's target, verified with llama-server --list-devices before trusting anything.

The trap: my distro's ROCm (7.2.4) segfaulted against my kernel (7.1.9). The forums call this "just downgrade" or "just upgrade." I did neither — I installed a nightly TheRock (community ROCm) userspace into a side directory and pointed LD_LIBRARY_PATH at it per model. System ROCm stays untouched; the model gets its own ROCm. When the next update breaks something, exactly one environment variable changes.

The four traps that cost me a month

  1. ROCm ↔ kernel version roulette. Fix: side-by-side userspace, as above.
  2. HIP index renumbering. Binding a second GPU after boot renumbers indexes; my 27B silently pointed at the 16 GB card and OOM'd a 20.9 GB allocation. Fix: pin HIP_VISIBLE_DEVICES by UUID, never index.
  3. Load-time bands. Measured, not guessed: warm 11 s, cold 62 s, cold-with-another-model-resident 127 s, and >300 s when available memory drops below ~20 GiB (the kernel evicts pages as fast as they fault in). My health-check timeout was inside the slow band, so a third of my loads were being killed and retried, compounding the problem. Timeouts are arithmetic, not vibes.
  4. Thermals. As shipped, a passively-cooled VDI card under sustained inference sits at 99–100 °C junction — and it has no fan interface at all: its hwmon node exposes no fan* or pwm* attributes, so software can neither watch it nor cool it. The fix is mechanical; see the next section.

The cooling fix (full BOM, about $76)

The first attempt was the obvious one: a 120 mm fan shroud adapter off eBay, the bolt-on kind that lets you point a case fan at a passive server card. It was wholly inadequate. Under sustained inference the junction climbed right back to the 99–100 °C plateau it sat at with nothing on it at all. One 120 mm fan cannot push enough static pressure through a Navi 21 fin stack to cool a card that was designed to have a server blower forced through it.

So I stopped cooling it like a desktop card and cooled it like server hardware: 4× 40 mm server blower fans on a shroud that seals the card's intake to the fan wall. The shroud is not my design — it's Caleb Szalacinski's AMD shroud on Printables, which covers the V340, V520, V620 and most of the Instinct line. Designing my own would have been an idiot move, and I don't own a printer, so the print cost me $30.

Two assembly details that matter more than they sound. Heat-set M3 inserts in the shroud, not self-tapping screws into PLA — you will open this thing more times than you expect. And 95 mm M3 screws running through the whole stack, so shroud, card, and fan wall come off as one assembly instead of five surprises.

The setback worth hearing before you buy: the PWM fans arrived with the wrong plugs. I found a wiring diagram online, trusted it, and fried both fans the first time power hit them — then damaged the shroud prying the assembly apart to get at them. Repaired with J-B Weld KwikWeld, finished with spare 2-wire fans wired correctly. Check the pinout against the fan's own datasheet and the plug against your controller before you apply power. A thumbnail off a forum is not a wiring document.

A shroud with gaps is a fan decoration; the sealant is what makes it a cooling solution. The controller lives outside software entirely, which is correct for a card that refuses to talk about its own temperature.

Budget a repaste into this as well, because you are buying a six-year-old card secondhand. I used a PTM7950 sheet ($17) plus 100 g of thermal putty ($45), and that is the one line item I'd buy differently: I used 15–20 g, so $18 of 30 g would have covered the job with plenty left over. A realistic first build doing the thermal work properly lands around $111.

And because the card won't watch itself, a watchdog does: a monitor script reads every thermal sensor every five seconds, asks the model server to unload when a card runs hot, and will trip a poweroff if things get truly bad. It has a real trip history — this isn't a hypothetical safety system, it's one that has actually saved this box from itself. The chassis is cardboard; the fire suppression strategy is software, and it has receipts.

Honest caveats

  • Inference only. This is not a training card and I won't pretend otherwise.
  • ROCm is a supported path, not a first-class one. Every driver/ROCm/llama.cpp update is a re-test. Budget for it.
  • Prefill loses to NVIDIA. Decode doesn't, by much. If your workload is 100K-token prompts all day, buy the other card.
  • The card needs airflow you engineer yourself — the shroud BOM above is cheap and non-optional.
  • If you need everything to work out of the box, this blog post is not for you. It's for the other 10,000 people.
